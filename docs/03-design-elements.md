# The Eight Essential Project Design Elements

The properties that make a project Gold Standard PBL rather than an activity with a rubric attached. This is the most important file in the repo.

Machine-readable version with check questions and failure modes: [`framework/design-elements.yaml`](../framework/design-elements.yaml). `pbl review` checks a plan against all eight.

> **On the count.** PBLWorks names seven. We split *reflection* and *critique & revision* into two, because they're planned separately, scheduled separately, and fail differently. Not a disagreement with the framework — a finer grain for checking a plan.

---

## 1. Key Knowledge, Understanding & Success Skills

**What it is.** The project teaches specific content derived from standards, plus one explicitly named success skill.

**Check:**
- Are the standards specific, and does answering the driving question require them?
- Is exactly *one* success skill named, with the dimensions being taught?
- Is the project focused, or trying to cover a semester?

**How it fails.** The project covers everything and teaches nothing in depth. Or the standards were chosen *after* the activity was designed, to justify it — you can always find a standard that sort of fits, and that isn't alignment.

**The one-skill rule.** Every project involves all five success skills. Teaching one is a different act. Name five and you'll teach none, because explicit teaching takes time you only have once. Pick one, pick one to three dimensions inside it, go deep. The schema makes a second skill structurally impossible.

## 2. Challenging Problem or Question

**What it is.** A real problem to solve or question to explore, expressed as an open-ended driving question in language the learner would actually use.

**Check:**
- More than one defensible answer?
- Would they understand and care about it without translation?
- Is it a challenge, or a topic wearing a question mark?

**How it fails.** The question is googleable. Or it names a subject — "What is a watershed?" — instead of posing a problem.

**The test.** Could you google the answer? Then it's a research prompt.

> ✗ "What are the parts of a watershed?"
> ✓ "How can we stop our driveway washing into the creek?"

→ [Driving questions, in depth](07-driving-questions.md)

## 3. Sustained Inquiry

**What it is.** An iterative process over weeks. The learner asks questions, finds resources, asks better questions, builds answers. Not a single research session.

**Check:**
- Does inquiry run across all four phases, or only at the start?
- Do their questions drive what happens next?
- More than one mode of research — reading, doing, asking people?

**How it fails.** Front-loading. If you teach all the content before the project starts, there's nothing left to inquire into and the project became dessert in week one.

This is the hardest element to accept, because teaching first *feels* responsible. It isn't: the learner should meet the problem before they have the tools, generate questions, and get instruction when they need it.

→ [Project Path](05-project-path.md) · [Need-to-know questions](10-strategies/need-to-know.md)

## 4. Authenticity

**What it is.** A connection to the real world through any combination of:

- **Real context** — the problem resembles one someone is actually paid to solve
- **Real processes and tools** — the methods, instruments, and quality standards practitioners use
- **Real impact** — something could genuinely change
- **Personal authenticity** — it connects to what this learner actually cares about

Any one is enough. More is better.

**Check:**
- Whose actual job does this resemble?
- Are the tools and quality standards the real ones?
- Could this matter to someone outside the household?
- Does it connect to something they already care about?

**How it fails.** "Authentic" means the worksheet mentions a career. Or the personal connection is asserted — "connects to student interests" — rather than named.

**In this repo, personal authenticity is enforced.** The plan's `authenticity.personal_connection` must name a specific interest from the learner profile. `pbl review` fails a generic claim. It's the check that catches a generic project with a child's name pasted on.

**Honesty about impact.** "Nothing changes, but this is genuinely how the work is done" is a real answer. Write that rather than inventing impact — invented stakes are transparent to learners and corrosive.

## 5. Learner Voice & Choice

**What it is.** Meaningful decisions about topic, questions, resources, product, audience, and use of time.

**Check:**
- Are these decisions an adult professional would actually make?
- Is the amount matched to this learner's appetite and experience?
- Does choice widen as the project goes on?

**How it fails.** Choice means picking a poster colour. Or a learner who freezes under ambiguity gets handed a blank page and called empowered.

**Calibration, not maximisation.** This is where the classroom framework and one-learner reality diverge most. A class teacher restricts choice because thirty projects are unmanageable. You have no such constraint — so your risk is *too much* open space. Low choice appetite means a short menu that widens, not no choice.

**The fake choice.** With one learner it's very easy to "offer" a choice you've already decided and steer until they pick it. They know. It's worse than no choice, because it teaches that their preferences are decorative.

→ [Facilitating choice](10-strategies/student-choice.md)

## 6. Reflection

**What it is.** Structured, repeated thinking about three things — **content** (what we're learning), **process** (how we're working), **purpose** (why it matters) — during the project.

**Check:**
- At least two reflection points, in different phases?
- Does it cover process and purpose, not just content recall?
- Is it ungraded?

**How it fails.** One reflection worksheet on the last day. Or reflections get graded, at which point the learner writes what you want to read and you've lost the only honest channel you had.

Most people only ever reflect on content. Process and purpose are where the transferable learning is.

→ [Reflection](10-strategies/reflection.md) · [Post-project reflection](10-strategies/post-project-reflection.md)

## 7. Critique & Revision

**What it is.** Structured feedback the learner acts on, with time scheduled for the revision. At least one full cycle before the work goes public.

**Check:**
- Is there a critique cycle before the presentation, with revision time after it?
- **Who gives it?** In a solo setting, is that person named?
- Is the feedback anchored to criteria rather than taste?

**How it fails.** Feedback arrives with the final grade, when revision is impossible. Or there's nobody to give it, and it quietly doesn't happen.

**This is the element most affected by having one learner.** There is no class. Decide who — a sibling, a grandparent, a co-op, an online group, an expert, or you deliberately in a different role — and write them into `support_plan.critique_partners`. Unnamed critique partners mean no critique.

**Revision gets its own calendar block.** Not folded into "finish the model," where it disappears.

→ [Critique protocols](10-strategies/critique-protocols.md) · [Revision](10-strategies/revision.md)

## 8. Public Product

**What it is.** The work goes to real people beyond the household or tutoring pair, and the learner explains their reasoning to them.

**Check:**
- Are the audience members named, specific, real people?
- Does the learner have to explain their thinking, not just display an object?
- If they're anxious about audiences, is there a ramp rather than a retreat?

**How it fails.** The audience is "the family." Or it got dropped because the learner is shy.

**Never drop it.** This is the most commonly abandoned element and the most costly. A public audience is what raises the quality bar — not through pressure, but because the learner can finally imagine a reader. For an anxious learner, build a **comfort ramp**: trusted adult → small friendly group → real audience. Fade the ladder, not the audience.

**"The community" is not an audience.** "The watershed council's monthly meeting" is. If you can't name who's coming, you don't have one yet.

## Pre-launch checklist

- [ ] Standards are specific, and answering the driving question requires them
- [ ] **One** success skill, with 1–3 named dimensions, and a plan for *teaching* it
- [ ] Driving question is open-ended and not googleable
- [ ] Inquiry runs across all four phases; content is not front-loaded
- [ ] Authenticity names a specific interest from the profile
- [ ] Choices are real, calibrated to this learner, and widen
- [ ] Two-plus reflection points, in different phases, ungraded
- [ ] A critique cycle before the presentation, with **named partners** and its own revision block
- [ ] A **named, specific, real** audience — with a comfort ramp if needed
- [ ] Every need in the profile has a scaffold, and every scaffold has a fade plan
