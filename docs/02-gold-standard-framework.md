## Design elements versus teaching practices

The distinction is worth holding onto, because they fail differently.

**Design elements** are properties of the plan. You can check them before the project starts, on paper, which is exactly what [`pbl review`](../schema/README.md) does. A missing design element is a design problem — fixable in an afternoon at a desk.

**Teaching practices** are properties of your behaviour during the project. No document can verify them. A project can be beautifully designed and run badly: you rescue too fast, you answer questions you should have deflected, you let the critique slide. That's a practice problem, and the only tool for it is honest reflection.

Most disappointing projects are well-designed and under-run. That's why the [teaching rubric](../rubrics/project-based-teaching-rubric.md) exists and why the [post-project reflection](10-strategies/post-project-reflection.md) matters more than it looks.

## Learning goals sit in the middle

Both halves point at the same centre:

**Key knowledge and understanding** — specific content standards, plus the bigger understanding you want to survive a year. Not coverage. A project that touches twelve standards teaches none of them properly.

**One success skill** — collaboration, critical thinking, creativity, complex communication, or self-directed learning. One. With one to three named dimensions inside it.

The one-skill rule is the most frequently broken and most consequential constraint in the whole framework. Every project *involves* all five skills. That's different from teaching one. If you name five, you will teach none, because there's no time to teach a skill explicitly five times over. The schema in this repo makes a second skill structurally impossible for exactly this reason.

## The three foundations must align

Underneath everything, three things have to fit together:

```
        LEARNING GOALS
         ↗          ↖
        /            \
DRIVING QUESTION ←→ PUBLIC PRODUCT
```

- Answering the **driving question** must *require* learning the **standards**. If a learner could answer it without learning them, the standards are decoration.
- The **product** must *demonstrate* the standards. If it doesn't, the assessment is fake regardless of how good the rubric is.
- The **question** must make the **product** the natural thing to make. If the product feels bolted on, it will be treated as bolted on.

Get these three aligned before you plan anything else. It's most of the work, and everything downstream depends on it — which is why [`CLAUDE.md`](../CLAUDE.md) tells an agent to stop and confirm them before mapping a single milestone.

## The Project Path runs through it

Four phases carry the project from launch to culmination: **Launch → Build Knowledge → Develop & Critique → Present.** All four appear in every project, including a two-week one. [Detail here](05-project-path.md).

## Where this repo diverges

Two deliberate departures, both flagged so nobody thinks we miscounted:

**We list eight design elements where PBLWorks lists seven.** We treat *reflection* and *critique & revision* as separate elements. They're planned separately, scheduled separately, and fail in different ways — reflection dies by being left to the last day, critique dies by having nobody to give it. Splitting them made the review checks sharper.

**We put the learner before the project.** In the classroom framework, knowledge of learners is one of four equity levers applied to a project designed for a class. Here it's the input: you write a [learner profile](15-learner-profiles.md) first, and the project is designed outward from it. The equity levers still apply — they just have something specific to act on.
