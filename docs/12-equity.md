# Equity-Centered PBL

Gold Standard PBL is designed to work for *all* learners, not the ones for whom school already works. PBLWorks names four levers. Each of them lands differently when you're teaching one person.

---

## Lever 1 — Knowledge of the learner

Know their strengths, interests, background, and life, and use that to make actual instructional decisions.

**In this setting you have more of this than any classroom teacher ever will.** The whole repo is built on it — the [learner profile](15-learner-profiles.md) is the primary input.

**So the question isn't whether you know them. It's what you do with it.**

Deep knowledge of a learner can raise the ceiling or lower it, and the second is more common than people admit. Knowing exactly where someone struggles makes it very easy to design around the struggle permanently — to route past reading forever, to never ask for the presentation, to keep choices narrow because open ones went badly once.

The check: **name one thing in this project that you know will be hard for them, and that you're doing anyway.** If there isn't one, you've used your knowledge to lower the ceiling.

---

## Lever 2 — Cognitive demand

High expectations for everyone, with real support. Scaffolding is how you *keep* demand high, not how you reduce it.

The distinction in practice:

| Reducing demand | Keeping demand, adding support |
|---|---|
| Simplify the concept | Simplify the sentence, keep the concept |
| Only offer the easy text | Offer three levels, learner chooses, expect them to climb |
| Skip the presentation | Build a comfort ramp to the presentation |
| Accept the first draft | Critique the first draft, schedule the revision |
| Do the hard part for them | Give them a procedure for being stuck |

The first column is faster and feels kind. It teaches the learner where their ceiling is.

**Exemplars are the most reliable way to raise demand.** Pick them slightly above what you think the learner can do. It costs nothing and it sets the aspiration. → [Models of quality work](10-strategies/models-of-quality-work.md)

**And every scaffold fades.** A support that never comes off is a permanent statement about what the learner can't do. → [`scaffolds.yaml`](../framework/scaffolds.yaml)

## Lever 3 — Literacy

Deliberate reading, vocabulary, and writing support inside every project, whatever the subject.

Not "the ELA part." A science project has a literacy load — the sources, the technical vocabulary, the written recommendation — and pretending otherwise means a learner who reads below grade level gets a science project they can't access.

**Three moves that do most of the work:**

1. **Tiered sources with the learner choosing the entry point.** Choice is what preserves agency; only offering the easiest tier is a ceiling, not a scaffold.
2. **Concept before word.** Do the thing, then name it. → [Academic vocabulary](10-strategies/academic-vocabulary.md)
3. **Read aloud, and pick the harder text.** Reading aloud gives access to vocabulary above the decoding level, and one-on-one there's no audience to feel self-conscious in front of.

**Include one adult-level source, read together.** For a learner who has concluded they're bad at reading, being the person who understands the hardest document in the room — with support — is a different experience from always getting the simplified version. That reframing is often worth more than the reading gain.

**Write a language objective**, alongside the content goals: *"uses permeable, runoff, sediment, and erosion correctly in explaining her recommendation, unprompted."* Assessable, and it's what the domain-vocabulary standards actually ask for.

## Lever 4 — Shared power

Voice and choice are not motivational garnish. They're how a learner develops agency, which means withholding them has a cost.

**What sharing power actually looks like:**

- **Co-construct the criteria.** In a class this is a facilitation feat; here it's a twenty-minute conversation after looking at exemplars, and it's the single biggest available upgrade to a solo project. → [Rubrics](10-strategies/rubrics.md)
- **Agree the norms rather than setting them.**
- **Let them choose the product**, from a menu filtered to their modes. Not the product you'd pick.
- **Let them decide what to do with feedback.** Choosing which critique to act on is part of the skill.

**And the honesty requirement.** With one learner it's very easy to "offer" a choice you've already decided and steer until they pick it. They know, and it teaches that their preferences are decorative — the opposite of shared power. If you need it to go a particular way, say so plainly and find something else to make genuinely open.

**Calibrated, not maximised.** Handing a blank page to a learner who freezes at ambiguity isn't empowerment. Low `choice_appetite` means a short menu that widens. → [Facilitating choice](10-strategies/student-choice.md)

## Equity in homeschool, microschool, and tutoring settings

Some things that are specific to this context.

**You may be the reason they're here.** Many learners in one-on-one settings left a classroom that wasn't working — sometimes because of a disability, sometimes because of how they were treated. That history shapes what they believe about themselves as a learner, and it's usually more of an obstacle than the original difficulty. The `goals_from_adult` field in the profile is often really about this.

**No peer comparison, in both directions.** Nobody is being measured against thirty others, which is a relief for a learner who always came bottom. It also removes the reference point that tells you whether the work is any good. Exemplars and outside voices are the substitutes. → [Assessment](09-assessment.md)

**Community access is uneven, and it's a real equity issue.** A project that assumes a car, a library card, reliable internet, or a parent with professional contacts advantages some learners over others. The `constraints` and `context.resources` fields exist so the plan is built on what's actually available. A project that can't happen isn't rigorous, it's just unachievable.

**Cultural and linguistic assets.** A bilingual learner has research access a monolingual one doesn't — they can interview people, read sources, and reach audiences you can't. That's an asset to design around, not a gap to remediate. The WIDA and WIDA-DALE standards are in the bundled set for exactly this. → [Standards](16-standards.md)

**Isolation is the real risk.** The single most common equity failure in solo settings isn't low expectations — it's a learner who only ever encounters one adult's ideas and one adult's judgment. Outside experts, critique partners, and a real audience are not enrichment here. They're the correction. → [Outside experts](10-strategies/outside-experts.md)

## Checklist

- [ ] Name one thing you know is hard for them that you're doing anyway
- [ ] The exemplars are above their current level
- [ ] Every scaffold has a fade plan
- [ ] The project has a literacy plan, whatever the subject
- [ ] There's a language objective, not just content goals
- [ ] At least one criterion was co-constructed
- [ ] The choices are real, not steered
- [ ] The plan is built on resources they actually have
- [ ] At least one voice in this project isn't yours
