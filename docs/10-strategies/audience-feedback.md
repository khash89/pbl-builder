# Audience Feedback

**Purpose:** Give the audience a real job, and get responses the learner can actually use.

Without a form, an audience claps politely and says "well done." With one, they engage with the content — and the learner gets something to think about instead of a general good feeling.

## Where it goes

**Present.** Design it during Develop & Critique.

## Design principles

- **Short.** Four questions. A long form gets fewer responses and worse ones.
- **Require engagement with the content.** "What did you learn?" makes them think about the substance. "Did you enjoy it?" doesn't.
- **Make it easy to complete.** Paper on the chair works. A QR code works if the audience is that kind of audience.
- **Have the learner design and revise it.** The form is itself a small public product — it goes to real people and it has to work. Critique it before the event.
- **Match it to the stage.** A work-in-progress exhibition wants "what's missing?"; a final presentation wants "what did you take away?"

## Questions that work

- What did you learn, or what did this make you think about?
- What was the strongest part?
- What still isn't clear to you?
- Do you have a question about the topic or about how the work was done?
- Anything else?

The third one is the most useful and the most often left out.

## Afterwards

Read them **with** the learner, not for them. Then:

- What's the pattern? Three people confused by the same thing is information.
- What surprised you?
- What would you change if you did this again?

Feed it into the [post-project reflection](post-project-reflection.md).

## With one learner

**A small audience makes every response count.** Eight forms from a council meeting, read carefully, is more useful than a hundred skimmed. Read every one properly.

**Ask the audience to sign them, if they're willing.** In a small setting, "the woman who monitors the creek upstream said my measurement method was sound" is a specific piece of praise a learner carries for a long time. Anonymous is safer in a class; named is better here.

**Handle the empty room honestly.** Sometimes three people turn up. That's still a public product — three real people who weren't obliged to come. Don't apologise for the number, and don't inflate it either.

**Ask one question they can't answer easily.** With a small, invested audience you can ask something substantive — "what would you want to know before acting on this recommendation?" — and get a real answer. That answer is often the most valuable single artifact of the project.

**You fill one in too.** Same form, as an audience member, honestly. It separates your assessor role from your audience role, and the learner reads it differently.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Two questions, or stickers with faces plus one spoken comment recorded by an adult.
- **3–5:** Three or four written questions, designed with support.
- **6–12:** Learner designs and revises it fully. Can include a rating scale plus open questions, and analyse the results.

## Common mistakes

- **No form.** Then the audience says "well done" and the learner learns nothing.
- **Too long.** Fewer responses, less thought in each.
- **Only satisfaction questions.** Tells you about the event, not the work.
- **Never reading them with the learner.** The reading-together is where the value lands.
- **Not designing it until the day before.** It's a product; critique it.
