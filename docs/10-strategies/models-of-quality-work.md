# Models of Quality Work

**Purpose:** Make "good" concrete before the learner starts, by analysing real examples.

The highest-value strategy for any learner who freezes at an open task — because "make something good" is unbearable without knowing what good looks like.

## Where it goes

**Launch** (build the criteria) and **Develop & Critique** (return to them).

## How to run it

1. **Find two to four real examples** of the product type. Professional work, published work, or strong work by past learners. Two to four, not one — a single exemplar gets copied.

2. **Include a mediocre one.** This is the move most people miss. Comparison teaches far more than excellence alone: a learner who can say why one is weaker understands the criteria in a way that admiring three good ones never produces.

3. **They analyse, you don't explain.** Gallery walk, or spread them out. The question is "what makes this one work?" — not "notice how the author…"

4. **Build the criteria list from what they noticed.** In their words. Post it on the [project wall](project-wall.md).

5. **Turn that list into the rubric**, or into the product criteria in the plan. Criteria the learner helped write are criteria they can actually use.

6. **Come back to the exemplars mid-build.** "Which of these is yours closest to, and what's the gap?" is one of the most useful questions in the project.

## Where to find exemplars

Real ones, ideally — the actual thing professionals produce. Published infographics, real policy submissions, actual podcast episodes, museum labels photographed on a phone. For most product types in [product-types.yaml](../../framework/product-types.yaml) the real-world version is findable in ten minutes.

Past learner work is good too, with permission and anonymised.

## With one learner

Works directly, and the analysis conversation is better one-on-one than in a group — you can follow their actual noticing rather than managing thirty hands.

**Two specific advantages:**

- **You can pick exemplars for this learner.** A class needs exemplars that work for everyone. You can choose three that are specifically calibrated to what this learner is drawn to and where they're likely to aim too low.
- **The mediocre example can be yours.** Making a deliberately weak version yourself, and having them critique it, is disarming and effective. It also models that first attempts are allowed to be bad.

**Watch the ceiling.** With one learner it's tempting to choose exemplars at their current level so they feel capable. Choose slightly above. The exemplar sets the aspiration, and it's the cheapest possible way to raise it.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Two examples, one obviously stronger. "Which one tells you more about the animal?"
- **3–5:** Three examples, criteria generated with support. They can spot features reliably.
- **6–12:** Four examples including one professional and one flawed. They can co-construct a full rubric.

## Common mistakes

- **One exemplar.** Gets copied rather than analysed.
- **You explaining the features.** Then the criteria are yours and they can't use them.
- **Only excellent examples.** No contrast, no learning.
- **Never returning to them.** They're a mid-project tool as much as a launch tool.
- **Choosing exemplars at the learner's current level.** Sets the ceiling at the floor.
