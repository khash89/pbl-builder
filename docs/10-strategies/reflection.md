# Reflection

**Purpose:** Structured thinking about the work, during the work.

Dewey's formulation is the one worth keeping: we don't learn from experience, we learn from reflecting on experience. Reflection is not a nice ending — it's the mechanism by which the project becomes knowledge that transfers.

## Where it goes

**All four phases.** At least two reflection points, in at least two different phases. Reflection only at the end is a summary.

## Three things to reflect on

| Focus | The question | Sample prompts |
|---|---|---|
| **Content** | What are we learning? | How did that change what you thought? How does this connect to the thing you found last week? |
| **Process** | How are we working? | What's slowing you down? What would you do differently tomorrow? Which strategy actually worked? |
| **Purpose** | Why does this matter? | How does this get us closer to the driving question? Who does this matter to? |

Most people only ever do content. Process and purpose are where the transferable learning is.

## How to run it

Pick a structure and keep it — the format should become invisible so the thinking is the only work.

- **Exit ticket** — two or three sentences at the end of a session
- **Learning log entry** — see [learning-log.md](learning-log.md)
- **Triangle-Square-Circle** — three key points / something that squares with what I thought / something still going round in my head
- **Think-pair-share** — needs a second person
- **"I used to think… now I think"** — the best one for showing shift, and ideal at the end

## Do not grade it

This matters more than it sounds. A graded or corrected reflection immediately becomes a performance: the learner writes what you want to read, and you lose the only honest channel you had.

Respond instead. Affirm the new realisation. Ask one probing question. That's it.

## With one learner

**Written reflection can't carry it alone**, especially for a learner who resists writing. Alternatives that work:

- **Walking conversation.** Genuinely effective — side by side, no eye contact, moving. Many learners say things walking that they'd never write.
- **Voice memo.** Same prompts, spoken, thirty seconds.
- **Drawing.** For younger learners, draw what changed.

**Delay the final reflection.** Not the same evening as the presentation — the learner is too flooded to say anything real. Two days later gets you the honest version.

**Reflect yourself, out loud, first.** With one learner your modelling is the whole culture. Saying "I got the sequence wrong in week two and it cost us three days" does more for their reflective honesty than any prompt.

**The risk here is drift into chat.** With one learner, reflection can dissolve into pleasant conversation that leaves no trace. Keep a written or recorded artifact, even a short one, or you can't tell in week four whether the thinking moved.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Verbal, whole-conversation, with drawing. Two prompts maximum.
- **3–5:** Short written or spoken. Introduce process reflection, not just content.
- **6–12:** All three focuses. They can select their own structure, and should by the end.

## Common mistakes

- **Only at the end.** Then it's a summary, not reflection.
- **Only content.** Loses the transferable part.
- **Grading it.** Kills honesty immediately.
- **Same prompt every time.** Becomes a ritual with no thought in it.
- **No record.** Especially in solo settings, where the conversation feels sufficient at the time.
