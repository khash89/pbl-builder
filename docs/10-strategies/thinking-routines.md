# Thinking Routines

**Purpose:** Short, repeatable protocols that make thinking visible.

Thinking routines are the work of [Project Zero](https://pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines) at the Harvard Graduate School of Education. They are free, well documented, and worth going to the source for — this page covers *when* to use which, in a project.

## Where they go

All four phases, with different routines suited to each.

## Matching routine to phase

**Launch — exploring and provoking questions**

| Routine | What it does |
|---|---|
| See-Think-Wonder | Close observation of a stimulus, then inference, then questions. The default for an [entry event](entry-event.md) built on an image, object, or place. |
| Zoom In | Reveals part of an image at a time. Excellent for generating questions before the whole picture is known. |
| Think-Puzzle-Explore | Surfaces current understanding and curiosity. Good just before need-to-knows. |
| Generate-Sort-Connect-Elaborate | Builds a concept map. Useful when the topic has many parts. |

**Build Knowledge — making sense of information**

| Routine | What it does |
|---|---|
| Sentence-Phrase-Word | Pulls what mattered out of a text at three scales. Good for a text that's slightly too hard. |
| +1 Routine | Recall and build, in rounds. Strong after a guest speaker or a video. |
| What Makes You Say That? | Demands evidence for a claim. The single most useful routine for critical thinking. |
| The 4Cs | Connections, challenge, concepts, changes — for a substantial text. |

**Develop & Critique**

| Routine | What it does |
|---|---|
| Chalk Talk | Silent written conversation on paper. Everyone contributes; no one dominates. |
| Micro-lab Protocol | Timed equal turns. Useful when one voice tends to take over. |

**Present**

| Routine | What it does |
|---|---|
| I Used to Think… Now I Think | Shows shift in understanding. The best closing reflection there is. |

## How to introduce one

1. Teach the routine explicitly, on low-stakes content.
2. Use it repeatedly until the structure is invisible.
3. Hand over the choice — eventually the learner picks the routine for the thinking they need.

A routine used once is an activity. A routine used ten times is a habit of mind, which is the actual point.

## With one learner

Several routines are built for group dynamics, and those need adapting or dropping.

**Work as-is:** See-Think-Wonder, Zoom In, Think-Puzzle-Explore, Sentence-Phrase-Word, What Makes You Say That?, I Used to Think… Now I Think.

**Need adapting:** Chalk Talk works with two people if you both genuinely write and neither speaks — and it's surprisingly good, because it removes your ability to lead by tone of voice. Micro-lab needs at least three.

**Don't bother with:** anything whose whole purpose is equalising participation across a large group. That problem doesn't exist here.

**The best solo use** is What Makes You Say That?, applied relentlessly, including to their own claims. It's a five-word intervention that does most of the work of the critical-thinking rubric.

**One caution:** with one learner, a routine can become a script you both perform. If the answers start sounding rehearsed, change routine.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** See-Think-Wonder, verbally, with drawing. One routine at a time, used often.
- **3–5:** Add Sentence-Phrase-Word and What Makes You Say That?
- **6–12:** All of them. They can select routines independently, which is the goal.

## Common mistakes

- **Using one once.** No habit forms.
- **Too many at once.** Pick two or three per project and use them properly.
- **Treating the output as the point.** The thinking is the point; the sticky notes are exhaust.
- **Group routines with one learner.** Wasted effort, and it feels artificial to everyone.
