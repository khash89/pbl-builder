# Academic Vocabulary

**Purpose:** Make the language of the domain usable, not just recognisable.

The difference matters. A learner who can pick "permeable" out of a multiple-choice list has recognition. A learner who says "the packed gravel isn't permeable, that's why it runs off" has usable vocabulary. Only the second one transfers.

## Where it goes

**Launch** and **Build Knowledge**, continuously.

## The five moves

**1. Concept before word.** Do something that demonstrates the idea, then name it. Watch water sink into loose soil and run off packed gravel — *then* learn "permeable." A word attached to an experience is retained; a word attached to a definition is memorised and lost. This is the highest-value move here.

**2. Frontload sparingly.** Some terms do need previewing before a text. Use images, gestures, analogies, and prior knowledge — not a definitions list.

**3. Model word-solving out loud.** Think aloud when you hit an unfamiliar word: what's around it, what's inside it, what would make sense. You're teaching a procedure, not a word.

**4. Read aloud, and stop at the terms.** Reading aloud lets you access text above the learner's decoding level, which is where the real vocabulary lives. Pause on the technical terms as they appear in context.

**5. Teach morphology.** Roots, prefixes, suffixes. *Hydro-*, *-logy*, *per-*, *im-*. One well-taught root unlocks a dozen words, which is a much better return than a dozen definitions.

## Record the language objective

Alongside your content goals, write what language the learner should be able to *use*:

> Uses *permeable*, *runoff*, *sediment*, and *erosion* correctly in explaining her recommendation, without prompting.

That's assessable, and it's what the standards about domain-specific vocabulary actually ask for.

## With one learner

**You hear every word they say.** In a class you catch a fraction of learners' speech; here you catch all of it. So assessment of usable vocabulary is direct — you know within a week whether "sediment" has become theirs or is still yours.

**The word wall must be theirs.** Terms added as encountered, in their own words, with a sketch. Never pre-populated by you. See the `word-wall-living` scaffold. A wall you filled in is a poster; a wall they filled in is a record of their own growing command of the domain.

**Read aloud without embarrassment.** For a learner reading below grade level, being read to is often the fastest route to grade-level vocabulary — and one-on-one, there's no audience to feel self-conscious in front of. Use it deliberately, and pick the harder text.

**Deliberately include one adult-level source.** Read together, aloud, with support. It lets the learner be the person who understands the hardest document available, which is a very different experience from always getting the simplified version. For a learner who has concluded they're bad at reading, this is the intervention.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Concept first, always. Pictures on the word wall. Repeat the term in context many times.
- **3–5:** Add morphology — prefixes and roots. Learner-maintained glossary.
- **6–12:** Full morphological analysis, plus attention to how the same word differs across disciplines.

## Common mistakes

- **Definitions first.** Produces recognition, not use.
- **A pre-made word wall.** Decorative.
- **Only assessing recognition.** Matching exercises don't tell you whether the word is usable.
- **Avoiding the real terms.** Learners can handle "permeability." Simplify the sentence, not the concept.
- **No language objective.** Then vocabulary is incidental and nobody checks it.
