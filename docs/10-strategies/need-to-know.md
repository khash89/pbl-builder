# Need-to-Know Questions

**Purpose:** Surface the learner's own questions, and use them to sequence instruction.

This is the mechanism that makes PBL inquiry rather than a themed unit. Instruction arrives because the learner needs it, not because it's Tuesday.

## Where it goes

**Launch** (generate) and **Build Knowledge** (drive). Revisit constantly.

## How to run it

Three columns, kept visible:

| KNOWS | NEED TO KNOWS | NEXT STEPS |
|---|---|---|
| What they already know | What they have to find out | How they'll start finding out |

1. **Launch first.** The [entry event](entry-event.md) has to happen before this, or you're asking them to be curious on command.

2. **Knows, 5 minutes.** Quick. This is warm-up, not assessment. Capture their exact words, including the wrong ones — you'll come back to those.

3. **Need-to-knows, 15 minutes.** Spend the time here. Push past the first three. When they stall, ask "what would you need to know to actually do this?" rather than "what else?"

4. **Next steps, 5 minutes.** This is where it gets real. "Do research" is not a next step. Push for: which source, which person, which measurement, by when.

5. **Post it. Revisit it.** Before a lesson, name which question it answers. After, mark what got answered and add what opened up. A need-to-know list that doesn't change is decoration.

## Sequencing instruction from it

This is the part people skip. Take the list and sort:

- **Which questions block progress?** Those get taught first.
- **Which will they answer themselves?** Get out of the way.
- **Which need an expert or a trip?** Those need lead time — book now.
- **Which are off-track but interesting?** A parking lot, honestly labelled. Some become the next project.

Then check: are your target standards represented? If not, you need a follow-up experience that provokes the missing question — not a lecture that answers a question nobody asked.

## With one learner

**The list gets short, and that's the risk.** One person generates five or six questions, not thirty. Two responses:

- **Add rounds rather than pressing harder.** Generate, research for twenty minutes, come back. New information reliably produces better questions than more prompting does. Three short rounds beats one long one.
- **Let yourself contribute — visibly.** With a class you'd never add your own questions. With one learner you can, as long as you label it: "here's one I'm wondering about, you don't have to take it." Marking whose question it is keeps the list theirs.

**Watch for the quiet substitution.** Over a few weeks the list drifts into being your objectives, reworded as questions. The tell is that every question now has a clean answer. Re-read it in week three and ask which of these they'd actually ask.

**They can't hide.** In a class a disengaged learner contributes nothing and it's absorbed. Here, a thin list is unambiguous signal — the launch didn't land. Believe it, and re-launch.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Whole-conversation, over several days. Use pictures. Simplify to three prompts: what I wonder about / what I think is true / how I could find out.
- **3–5:** Can generate independently but will need help getting past surface questions. Model one good one.
- **6–12:** Can categorise and prioritise their own list, which is itself worth teaching. They can also write the driving question from it.

## Common mistakes

- **Collecting once, never revisiting.** Then it's a warm-up activity, not an inquiry engine.
- **Accepting "do research" as a next step.** Vague plans produce vague work.
- **Front-loading anyway.** If you teach everything in week one, the list is irrelevant by week two.
- **Answering questions immediately.** Some of them are theirs to answer.
