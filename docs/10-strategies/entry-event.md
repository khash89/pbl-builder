# Entry Event

**Purpose:** Create a genuine need to know, before any content is taught.

An entry event is not an introduction to a project. It's an experience that leaves the learner with questions they want answered. If your first move is explaining the assignment, you haven't launched — you've announced.

## Where it goes

**Launch.** Day one, before the driving question is even named.

## How to run it

1. **Pick something the learner encounters directly.** Not a description of a problem — the problem. Go stand in it. Break something open. Meet someone. Watch the thing happen.

2. **Say nothing about the project.** Genuinely nothing. The experience has to land as an experience.

3. **Ask one question at the end.** One. Chosen so that answering it requires the standards you're targeting. Then stop talking.

4. **Capture what they ask.** Immediately, in their words, on paper. This becomes the need-to-know list. Resist correcting or reframing their questions — the wrong-sounding ones are often the most useful.

5. **Name the project after that.** Now the driving question makes sense, because they already want it answered.

Good entry events: a site visit while the problem is happening; a disassembled object; a conversation with someone affected; a piece of data that contradicts what they assumed; a genuinely puzzling artifact.

Weak entry events: a video about the topic; a KWL chart; a reading; you, explaining.

## Design it backwards

Write your anticipated need-to-know list *first*, then design an experience that would produce it. If your list and your standards don't overlap, the entry event points the wrong way and no amount of enthusiasm on the day will fix it.

## With one learner

You lose the room. Thirty kids generate momentum off each other; one kid does not. Two consequences:

- **The stimulus has to be stronger.** A video that would carry a class discussion will produce a shrug. Go somewhere, meet someone, get wet. Physical and social beat mediated, every time, and it's much easier to arrange for one person.
- **Write the questions down.** With a class the questions live on chart paper because there are too many to remember. With one learner you will both feel like you remember the conversation. You won't, and by week two the need-to-know list will have quietly become your objectives list instead of theirs.

The advantage: you can wait for the right moment. A class launch has to happen on the scheduled Monday. You can wait for the rain.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Break it across several days, with read-alouds between to let questions accumulate. Expect the questions to be concrete and specific — that's fine, they're still questions.
- **3–5:** One event, questions captured same day. They'll need help distinguishing "what I wonder" from "what I already know."
- **6–12:** Can handle a genuinely ambiguous stimulus and often generate better questions with less scaffolding. Consider having them write the driving question themselves afterwards.

## Common mistakes

- **Explaining first.** The single most common failure. The moment you frame it, curiosity becomes compliance.
- **An entry event with no relationship to the standards.** Exciting, then a hard pivot into unrelated content. The learner notices.
- **Correcting their questions.** A question you didn't want is data about what they actually noticed.
- **Doing it once.** Re-launching mid-project is legitimate when inquiry stalls, and rarely done.
