# Critique Protocols

**Purpose:** Structured feedback the learner can actually act on, anchored to criteria rather than taste.

This is the strategy most often skipped and most responsible for quality. It is also the one that genuinely requires another person.

## Where it goes

**Develop & Critique.** At least one full cycle must complete *before* the work goes public — feedback that arrives with the final grade is not critique, because there's no time left to act on it.

## Two kinds

**Early, broad, low-stakes.** Multiple pieces of work, quick reactions, critic can be anonymous.
- *Gallery walk* — work displayed, written feedback left on sticky notes
- *Feedback carousel* — timed rounds of silent written comment

**Later, deep, single artifact.** One piece, sustained attention, maker present and responding.
- *Tuning protocol* — warm feedback, then cool feedback, maker listens without defending, then responds
- *Praise-Question-Suggestion* — one of each, in that order
- *Charrette* — rapid design iteration with the group

## How to run it

1. **Norms first.** Kind, specific, helpful. Not one of three — all three. Establish this before the first critique, not during.

2. **Anchor to criteria.** Read one criterion aloud, get feedback on that criterion, move on. Unanchored critique becomes taste, and taste feels personal.

3. **Practise on something that isn't theirs.** Critique an anonymous sample together first, or an exemplar. Ron Berger's "Austin's Butterfly" demonstration is the standard reference for showing what revision looks like — it's worth finding.

4. **Sentence starters.** "I noticed…", "I wondered why…", "Have you considered…". Not because learners can't think, but because the form is what keeps it kind under pressure.

5. **The maker listens, then responds.** Not defends. Listening without justifying is a taught skill and takes practice.

6. **Schedule the revision separately.** This is the step that gets dropped. Critique on Thursday, revision block on Friday, on the calendar, as its own work. Feedback with no revision time is just an evaluation.

7. **They choose what to fix.** Not everything. Choosing which feedback to act on is part of the skill.

## With one learner

**This is the strategy that needs other people.** There is no class. Decide who, before the project starts, and write them into `support_plan.critique_partners` — unnamed critique partners mean the critique quietly doesn't happen.

Who actually works:

- **A grandparent or family friend.** Excellent, and underused. They ask genuinely naive questions, which is exactly what tests clarity. Give them the criteria and permission to be confused.
- **A sibling.** Especially a younger one for anything that has to be understandable. They will tell you the truth.
- **A co-op or online group.** Best option if available — actual peers.
- **An outside expert.** Powerful in the later stages. A learner revises differently when a real practitioner is going to look.
- **You, deliberately, in a different role.** Legitimate but requires explicit framing: *"For the next twenty minutes I'm your critic, not your parent. I'm going to be picky about the criteria."* Say it out loud. Then come back out of role just as explicitly.

**Frame it a day early.** A learner who dislikes showing unfinished work — and most do — needs to know the critique is coming and that a first version is *supposed* to have problems. Sprung critique reads as ambush.

**The intimacy problem.** With one learner, criticism of the work and criticism of the person collapse into each other very easily, especially between parent and child. Two defences: anchor everything to written criteria that existed before the draft, and get at least one voice that isn't you.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** One criterion, two comments, heavy modelling. "One thing I liked, one thing I wondered."
- **3–5:** Praise-Question-Suggestion with sentence starters. Gallery walk works well.
- **6–12:** Full tuning protocol. They can facilitate it themselves, and should.

## Common mistakes

- **No revision time.** The most common and most costly.
- **Critique only at the end.** Then it's grading.
- **Nobody assigned to give it.** Especially in solo settings.
- **Praise-only.** Kind without helpful isn't critique.
- **Fixing it for them.** The hardest one to resist when you love the learner.
