# Facilitating Choice

**Purpose:** Offer real decisions, calibrated so they build agency rather than cause paralysis.

## Where it goes

**Launch, Build Knowledge, Develop & Critique.** Choice should widen as the project goes on.

## Three categories

**Content** — what to investigate, which angle, which real-world issue, which sub-topic.
**Process** — who to work with, which research method, how to organise the work, how to use time.
**Product** — what to make, what format, which audience, which tools.

The test for all three: **is this a decision an adult professional would actually make?** Choosing a font colour is not voice and choice. Choosing which of three designs to recommend is.

## Calibrating it

This is the part that matters, and it's why the learner profile carries a `choice_appetite` field.

| Appetite | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Low** | A menu of two or three concrete options, plus "or propose your own" always available. Widen each phase. |
| **Medium** | A wider menu, or an open choice with a deadline for deciding. |
| **High** | Get out of the way. Set the constraint, not the option. |

Low appetite does **not** mean no choice. It means a short menu — because an open field produces paralysis, and paralysis produces less agency, not more. The `choice-menu-narrowed` scaffold exists for exactly this, and it fades.

Also calibrate to **PBL experience**. A learner doing their first project needs narrower choices than the same learner on their fourth, regardless of appetite.

## Widening it

Write down what has to happen before you offer more. The plan schema has a `widens_when` field for this.

Good triggers: they've committed to one focus and stuck with it for a week; they've completed one full cycle of something; they've made a choice and dealt with a consequence of it.

Choice that stays fixed all project is not calibrated, it's just a setting.

## With one learner

**The genuine risk is the opposite of the classroom one.** A class teacher restricts choice because thirty different projects are unmanageable. You have no such constraint — which means the risk is *too much* open space, especially for a learner who reads ambiguity as threat.

**Watch for the fake choice.** With one learner it's very easy to "offer" a choice you've already decided, and steer until they pick it. They know. It's worse than no choice because it teaches that their preferences are decorative. If you need it to be a particular way, say so and choose something else to be genuinely open.

**Their choices will reveal the profile was wrong.** This is useful. A learner you pegged as low-appetite who immediately proposes something you didn't offer has told you something. Update the profile.

**Constrained resources are a legitimate limit, honestly stated.** "No car on weekdays, so it has to be within walking distance" is a real constraint and saying it plainly is better than pretending the choice is open and then vetoing.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Two options, both concrete, both visible. Choice of materials and of what to draw.
- **3–5:** Choice boards work well. Three or four options across content and product.
- **6–12:** Can write their own driving question and choose their own audience. Should be doing so by their second or third project.

## Common mistakes

- **Trivial choice.** Colour, font, title. Feels like choice, isn't.
- **Open field to a learner who freezes.** Then choice becomes the obstacle.
- **Steering to a predetermined answer.** Transparently fake.
- **Never widening.** The point is growth in agency, not a fixed dose.
- **Choosing the product for them.** The most common one. Build the menu, then step back.
