# Preparing for Presentations

**Purpose:** Build presentation skill deliberately, with rehearsal and — where needed — a comfort ramp.

## Where it goes

**Develop & Critique** (rehearsal) and **Present**.

## Start on day one

Tell the learner who they'll present to and when, at the launch. Not because it's motivating in the abstract, but because it changes how they work for six weeks. A learner who knows the planning commission is coming builds differently from a learner who finds out in week five.

## How to run it

1. **Define audience and format before the project starts.** Both go in the plan.

2. **Show real models.** Actual talks, actual pitches, actual museum tours. See [models-of-quality-work.md](models-of-quality-work.md). Include one that's mediocre.

3. **Scaffold the planning.** An outline, a storyboard, a presentation plan — see [`templates/student/presentation-plan.md`](../../templates/student/presentation-plan.md). Not a full script to read from.

4. **Rehearse three times, at least.** On the calendar, as separate sessions.

5. **Content feedback first. Delivery feedback later.** This ordering is not optional. Telling someone to make eye contact while the argument is still wrong wastes both rounds — and fixing the argument often fixes the delivery, because confidence follows clarity.

6. **Handle the logistics yourself.** Room, chairs, tech tested in the actual room, timekeeping. The learner should only have to think about the work. Set up before the audience arrives if the learner dislikes being watched setting up.

7. **Brief the audience.** Give them a role and a [feedback form](audience-feedback.md). An audience that knows it has a job asks better questions.

## The comfort ramp

For a learner with low audience comfort, the answer is never to drop the audience. The public product is the element doing the most work — it's what raises the quality bar. Build a ramp instead:

1. **Alone**, or to a pet, or to the wall. Genuinely useful.
2. **One trusted adult.** A grandparent, at the work, informally.
3. **A small friendly group.** Two or three people who like them.
4. **The real audience.**

Four rungs for a first-timer, three next time, one or two by the third project. Fade the ladder, not the audience.

Have a backup that is still public: if the meeting falls through or the learner can't do it on the night, the expert comes to the house. That's a real person with real interest who isn't family. Retreating to presenting to you is the thing to avoid.

## Presentation day checklist

- Date confirmed, guests know when and where
- Audience materials printed
- Room arranged
- Tech tested *in that room*
- Timekeeping ready
- Audience briefed on their role
- Water for the presenter
- Something to do with their hands if that helps

## With one learner

**The ramp is the whole strategy.** It's also easier to build than in a class — you can arrange four small audiences without timetabling anything.

**Rehearse to the object, not the room.** If the product is a model or an exhibit, have the learner walk someone through it physically. Standing next to a thing and pointing is a completely different experience from standing in front of people talking, and for a nervous learner it's the difference between possible and not.

**Record a rehearsal and watch it with the sound off.** Effective, and less exposing than live feedback on delivery.

**Cut, don't add.** When a rehearsal goes badly the instinct is to add — more notes, more slides, more prompts. Almost always the fix is less. A shorter thing said well beats a longer thing survived.

**Consider banning slides for a first talk.** Especially if the learner will hide behind them. The product is usually the visual.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Two minutes. Something to hold. Rehearse to a stuffed animal, then a grandparent. Family audience counts as public at this age if it extends beyond the household.
- **3–5:** Three to five minutes. Notes on a card, not a script. Two rehearsals minimum.
- **6–12:** Five to ten minutes plus questions. Rehearse the questions specifically — ask hostile ones.

## Common mistakes

- **Announcing the audience late.** Loses most of the motivational effect.
- **Delivery feedback before content is settled.** Wastes both rounds.
- **No rehearsal on the calendar.** Then there isn't one.
- **Dropping the audience because the learner is anxious.** The single most costly compromise in PBL.
- **Reading from slides.** Ban them for the first rehearsal and see what happens.
- **Not testing the tech in the room.** It will fail in the room.
