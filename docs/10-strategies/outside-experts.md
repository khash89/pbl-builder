# Working with Outside Experts

**Purpose:** Bring in someone who does this for real — for content, for critique, or as audience.

The most underused strategy in the set, and the one that most reliably raises quality. Most people say yes. Almost nobody asks.

## Where it goes

Any phase, doing different work in each.

| Phase | What the expert does |
|---|---|
| **Launch** | Answers the first need-to-knows, corrects early misconceptions, makes the problem real |
| **Build Knowledge** | Supplies content and method that isn't in any text; answers the specific question |
| **Develop & Critique** | Critiques the product. The knowledge that a real practitioner will look at it changes how a learner works. |
| **Present** | Part of the public audience; asks the questions that matter |

## How to find them

Closer than you think:

- Local government staff — planning, public works, environmental health. Their job includes public engagement.
- Local businesses and trades
- University faculty and graduate students — often keen, rarely asked
- Nonprofits, councils, community organisations
- Library staff, museum staff, historical societies
- Your own network: parents, neighbours, the person whose job came up at a barbecue
- Professional subreddits and forums, for a written exchange

## How to ask

Short email. Specific request. Named time commitment. What's in it for them stated honestly.

> My 9-year-old is investigating why our driveway washes into the creek and what to do about it. Would you be willing to spend 20 minutes on a video call in the week of the 12th answering her questions? She'll send them in advance. She's planning to present her recommendation to the watershed council in May.

The three things that make this get a yes: it's short, it's bounded, and it's obviously real.

## Preparing the expert

Ten minutes of prep changes the outcome completely:

- Send the learner's actual questions in advance
- Say what they know and don't know yet
- Send the criteria if you want them to critique against something
- Be explicit about level: "she reads below grade level but understands a lot — please don't simplify the ideas, just the words"
- Tell them what would be unhelpful: doing it for her, giving the answer

## Preparing the learner

This is where the learning is. See [interviews.md](interviews.md) — question preparation, follow-up questions, note-taking, and a rehearsal.

**The learner asks the questions. Not you.** Say in advance that you'll be silent, then be silent. It will be uncomfortable. Sit through it.

## With one learner

**This is the biggest single advantage of one-on-one PBL.** A class can't take thirty learners to a professional; you can arrange a twenty-minute video call for one. Experts who would never commit to a school visit will give one kid twenty minutes.

**It also solves the critique problem.** The hardest structural gap in solo PBL is finding someone other than you to critique the work. An expert critique in week three or four fills that gap and carries more weight than any peer would.

**And it solves the audience problem.** One confirmed expert is a legitimate public audience. It's not a downgrade from a room of thirty — arguably it's better, because they'll ask real questions.

**Book early and expect one to fall through.** Confirm in writing. Have a second option. The `resources.experts[].status` field in the plan schema exists because "I emailed someone" and "someone confirmed for the 14th" are different things and it's easy to conflate them in your head.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Short, concrete, in person if possible. Something to look at or hold. Ten minutes.
- **3–5:** Can prepare and ask their own questions with support. Video calls work.
- **6–12:** Can arrange the contact themselves — which is worth having them do — conduct a real interview, and act on professional critique.

## Common mistakes

- **Not asking.** By far the biggest.
- **You do the talking.** Then the learner watched two adults have a conversation.
- **No preparation.** Wastes the expert's time and the learner gets a lecture instead of answers.
- **Booking too late.** Real people have calendars.
- **Only using them as audience.** Their most valuable role is usually critique, mid-build, while there's time to act on it.
