# Revision Strategies

**Purpose:** Turn received feedback into work that is actually better.

Critique without revision is evaluation. This is the other half.

## Where it goes

**Develop & Critique**, scheduled as its own block immediately after a critique cycle.

## How to run it

1. **Reflect before revising.** Ten minutes between critique and revision: what did they hear, what surprised them, what do they disagree with. Revising straight from raw feedback produces compliance edits.

2. **They choose.** From all the feedback, they pick what to act on — and can defend not acting on some of it. Choosing is the skill; doing everything you were told is not revision, it's dictation.

3. **Name the revision.** "I'm changing X because Y said Z and I agree" gets written down before the change is made. This is what separates revision from fiddling.

4. **One pass, one focus.** Content revision, then a separate pass for clarity, then a separate pass for presentation. Trying to fix everything at once fixes nothing.

5. **Keep the old version.** Visible improvement is the most motivating thing in the project. A learner who can see draft one next to draft three understands what revision is for in a way no explanation achieves.

## Versions, not corrections

The framing matters enormously. "Draft two" implies the work is developing. "Corrections" implies it was wrong. Same activity, opposite message about whether the learner is capable.

Number the drafts. Date them. Put them side by side on the [project wall](project-wall.md).

## With one learner

**Protect the block.** With one learner and a flexible schedule, revision is the easiest thing to fold into "finishing the model" — at which point it disappears. Put it on the calendar as its own session with its own name.

**Watch for perfectionist paralysis and its opposite.** Some learners will revise the same paragraph for three days; some will declare it done at draft one. For the first, time-box it and require them to move on. For the second, require the specific change to be named and shown.

**Don't hold the pen.** The strongest pull in one-on-one teaching is to make the small fix yourself because it'd take ten seconds. Every time you do, you take the revision. Point at the thing, say what's unclear, and wait.

**Use the two-day gap.** Revision the day after critique is better than the same afternoon; two days later is often better still. A learner who has stopped being defensive revises more honestly. You have schedule flexibility a classroom doesn't — use it.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** One specific change, modelled. "Let's add the thing Grandma couldn't find."
- **3–5:** Two or three changes, chosen by them from a list. Keep drafts visibly.
- **6–12:** Full self-directed revision with a written rationale. They can run their own passes.

## Common mistakes

- **No scheduled time.** Then it doesn't happen.
- **Revising everything at once.** Produces a worse draft.
- **Discarding earlier drafts.** Throws away the evidence of growth.
- **Adult edits.** Fast, and it teaches the learner that their work isn't theirs.
- **Calling it corrections.** Small word, large effect.
