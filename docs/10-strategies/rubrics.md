# Using Rubrics

**Purpose:** Share the criteria before the work starts, and use them all the way through.

A rubric used only for grading is a scoring sheet. A rubric used from day one is an instructional tool.

## Where it goes

**All four phases**, doing different work in each.

| Phase | What the rubric does |
|---|---|
| **Launch** | Shared before any work. Analysed against [exemplars](models-of-quality-work.md). Ideally co-constructed. |
| **Build Knowledge** | Periodic self-check: where am I against this? What's missing? |
| **Develop & Critique** | Anchors the [critique](critique-protocols.md). Learner self-assesses before anyone else speaks. |
| **Present** | Learner self-assesses the finished work before receiving your assessment. |

## Three kinds in play

- **Product criteria** — derived from the content standards. What makes this specific artifact good.
- **Success skill rubric** — the one targeted skill, and only the named dimensions. See [rubrics/success-skills/](../../rubrics/success-skills/).
- **Project design rubric** — for you, not the learner. See [project-design-rubric.md](../../rubrics/project-design-rubric.md).

## How to make product criteria that work

**Derive them from the standards, not from neatness.** If the standard is about comparing multiple solutions, a criterion is "all three tested designs appear, including the ones that lost" — not "the poster is neat and colourful."

The test: could a learner meet every criterion and still not have learned the standard? Then the criteria are wrong.

**Keep it short.** Four or five criteria that matter beat twelve that cover everything. Every criterion is something you're committing to teach and assess.

**Write them so the learner can use them alone.** "Explains the reasoning" is not usable. "Can explain the three-step chain of what the water does, without notes" is.

## With one learner

**Co-construction is genuinely available here.** In a class it's a facilitation feat; with one learner it's a twenty-minute conversation after looking at exemplars. Criteria the learner helped write are criteria they'll actually self-assess against, and this is the single biggest upgrade available to a solo project.

**Self-assessment carries more weight.** There's no peer comparison, no class distribution, and one assessor who loves them. So the learner's own judgment against written criteria isn't a nice extra — it's a substantial part of the assessment, and it has to be taught deliberately. Have them score it and justify each score *before* you say anything.

**Guard against drift.** Assessing someone you watched struggle is hard to do consistently. The written criteria are your protection: score against the words, not against the effort you witnessed. Record effort separately if you want to — both matter, they're just different things.

**Get one outside voice on the final product.** An expert, a co-op teacher, the audience. Not because your judgment is bad, but because a second reading is the only real check available.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Two or three criteria, with pictures. Reviewed one at a time, out loud.
- **3–5:** Four criteria, learner self-assesses with support. Co-construct at least one.
- **6–12:** Full co-construction, independent self-assessment with written justification.

## Common mistakes

- **Handing it over with the grade.** Then it never taught anything.
- **Criteria about presentation rather than learning.** Neat and wrong scores well.
- **Too many criteria.** Nothing gets taught properly.
- **Assessing all five success skills.** One skill, one to three rows. Everything else is out of scope and should be said so.
- **You self-assess first.** Learner goes first, always.
