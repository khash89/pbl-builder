# Preparing & Conducting Interviews

**Purpose:** Teach interviewing as a real research method — not a formality before the real work.

## Where it goes

**Build Knowledge**, mainly. Also useful at Launch for understanding a client's or audience's actual needs.

## The skills to teach

- **Open questions.** "What's the hardest part of your job?" not "Do you like your job?"
- **Follow-ups.** The second question matters more than the first. "Why is that?" and "Can you give me an example?" are most of the skill.
- **Active listening.** Which mostly means not planning your next question while they're answering.
- **Silence.** Leave the pause. People fill it with the good material. This is the single hardest thing to teach and the highest-yield.
- **Notes or recording.** With permission, always asked explicitly.

## How to prepare

1. **What do we actually want to know?** Two or three goals, written. Not a topic — goals.
2. **Background research first.** Asking something findable on their website wastes the interview and signals you didn't bother.
3. **Draft questions**, then cut half. Six good questions beats fifteen.
4. **Order them.** Easy and factual first, the one that matters in the middle, open-ended at the end.
5. **Rehearse.** Actually role-play it, with someone playing the expert badly — giving one-word answers, going off-topic. The learner has to practise recovering.

## Afterwards

Debrief within a day:

- What were we trying to find out? Did we?
- What surprised you?
- What did you not get an answer to?
- What would you do differently?

Then write it up while it's fresh. A recording nobody transcribes is a recording nobody uses.

## With one learner

**Video calls make this cheap and repeatable.** No permission slips, no bus, no scheduling thirty people. Twenty minutes with a real practitioner is genuinely available to a solo learner in a way it isn't to a class.

**The hard part is you staying quiet.** You'll want to rescue the silences and clarify the awkward questions. Don't. Say beforehand: *"I'm not going to say anything after hello."* Then hold it. The three seconds where nobody speaks and the learner has to ask their own follow-up is where the skill actually forms.

**Rehearse the recovery, not the script.** With one learner you have time to run the interview twice with you as an unhelpful expert. Practise what to do when the answer is short, or off-topic, or above their head. That's what will actually happen.

**Interview family too.** A grandparent's account of how the creek used to flood is primary-source research. It's also low-stakes practice for the higher-stakes interview later.

**One interview can be the whole audience.** For a learner with low audience comfort, a real practitioner they've spoken to twice becomes an audience they can present to — and they've already had the hard first conversation.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Two questions, asked with an adult present, of someone familiar. Draw the answer.
- **3–5:** Four or five questions, prepared and rehearsed. Can take their own notes with a note-catcher.
- **6–12:** Full independent interview, including arranging it, following up, and quoting accurately. Teach attribution.

## Common mistakes

- **Questions with findable answers.** Wastes the interview.
- **Too many questions.** Turns it into a survey with no depth.
- **The adult talking.** Then it wasn't the learner's interview.
- **No follow-ups.** The whole value is in the second question.
- **No debrief.** The material never gets used.
- **Not asking permission to record.** Teach this properly; it's part of the professional practice.
