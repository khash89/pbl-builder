# Project Wall

**Purpose:** Make the project continuously visible, so the learner can orient without asking you.

## Where it goes

**All four phases.** Set it up at launch; it stays up until the project ends.

## How to run it

One surface, where the work happens. On it:

- The driving question, big, at the top
- The need-to-know list, updated
- The next deadline
- The product criteria
- Work in progress, including drafts
- Photos, data, measurements as they accumulate

Physical beats digital when the work is physical. A wall you walk past does something a document you have to open does not.

**The rule that makes it work:** if the learner asks you something that's on the wall, point at the wall instead of answering. Twice, and they start checking it.

## Why it earns its space

It offloads what working memory shouldn't hold, and it makes the project continuous across days. A learner returning after a weekend can rebuild context in ten seconds instead of asking you to.

It also makes drift visible. A wall that hasn't changed in a week is telling you something.

## With one learner

Works as-is, and it's arguably more valuable here — a class has thirty people carrying shared context, a solo learner has one.

Two adaptations:

- **Hand it over.** By week two, the learner maintains it, not you. That transfer is not administrative convenience; it's the self-management outcome. What they choose to put on it tells you what they think matters.
- **Keep it small.** A whole wall for one learner becomes archaeology. A single sheet of A3 or a whiteboard forces the editing that makes it useful. See the `single-page-project-wall` scaffold.

If the workspace is shared with family life, a portable version — a trifold board that folds away — works better than something permanent, and the ritual of opening it starts the session.

## By grade band

- **Pre-K–2:** Pictures and photographs over text. The driving question read aloud each time until they can say it.
- **3–5:** They can maintain it with prompting. Use it for a visible progress tracker.
- **6–12:** Fully learner-owned. A Kanban layout works well and teaches something transferable.

## Common mistakes

- **You maintain it.** Then it's your dashboard about them, not their tool.
- **Too much on it.** Everything visible means nothing visible.
- **Never referring to it.** If you don't point at it, it's wallpaper.
- **Digital when the work is physical.** A Padlet nobody opens is worse than a piece of paper on the fridge.
