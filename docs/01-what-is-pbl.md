# What Is Project Based Learning?

Project Based Learning is a teaching method in which a learner gains knowledge and skills by working for an extended period to investigate and respond to an authentic, engaging, complex question or problem — and produces something real for a real audience.

The phrase that captures it best is **the main course, not dessert** (Larmer & Mergendoller). The content is learned *through* the project. It is not taught first and then decorated with a poster.

## The distinction that matters

| Doing a project | Project Based Learning |
|---|---|
| Added on after the content is taught | The content is learned by doing the work |
| Adult-directed start to finish | Real decisions belong to the learner |
| One correct answer | Open-ended question with defensible alternatives |
| Assessed by the adult, privately | Public product, real audience |
| Reflection at the end, if at all | Reflection built in throughout |
| Feedback arrives with the grade | Critique and revision cycles during the work |

Both columns can produce a nice-looking artifact. Only one produces the learning.

The uncomfortable test: **if you removed the project, would the learner have learned the same content anyway?** If yes, it was dessert.

## Why it works

- **Retention.** Knowledge acquired in a meaningful context is retained longer than knowledge acquired for a test. The context is the retrieval cue.
- **Transfer.** A learner who has used a concept to solve something recognises it in a new situation. A learner who has only defined it usually doesn't.
- **Motivation that isn't extrinsic.** A real audience and a real problem supply a reason to care that no amount of enthusiasm from you can manufacture.
- **Quality rises when work goes public.** Reliably, and more than most teachers expect. The mechanism isn't pressure — it's that the learner can finally imagine a reader.
- **The success skills have somewhere to live.** You cannot teach critical thinking in the abstract. You think critically *about* something, and a project supplies the something.

## What it isn't

**It isn't unstructured.** Good PBL is more planned than a lecture sequence, not less. The plan just makes room for the learner's decisions.

**It isn't a rejection of direct instruction.** You teach — often, briefly, when the learner needs it. The difference is the timing: instruction arrives because a question demands it, not because it's Tuesday.

**It isn't only for enrichment.** The most common misuse is treating it as what you do with capable learners once the real curriculum is finished. The evidence points the other way: learners who struggle with abstraction often do better when the abstraction has a purpose attached.

**It isn't hands-on-equals-minds-on.** A learner can build an elaborate model and learn nothing. Activity is not thinking. The design elements are what make the difference.

## The eight elements, in one line each

A project is Gold Standard PBL when it has all of these. [Full detail here](03-design-elements.md).

1. **Learning goals** — specific standards, plus *one* named success skill
2. **A challenging problem or question** — open-ended, in the learner's language
3. **Sustained inquiry** — weeks, driven by their questions, iterative
4. **Authenticity** — real context, real tools, real impact, or real personal connection
5. **Voice and choice** — decisions an adult professional would actually make
6. **Reflection** — on content, process, and purpose, during the work
7. **Critique and revision** — feedback they act on, with time to act
8. **A public product** — real people beyond the household

Miss one and the project usually still works. Miss three and you have an activity with a rubric.

## What this repo adds

The framework above is [PBLWorks](https://www.pblworks.org/)' and it assumes a classroom. This repo assumes **one learner**.

That changes real things:

- **Critique needs a named person.** There's no class to provide it. If you don't decide who in advance, it won't happen. This is the single biggest structural difference.
- **Individual accountability stops being a problem.** The hardest assessment problem in classroom PBL — did this learner actually do anything? — evaporates. What replaces it is objectivity, because you are assessing someone you love.
- **Scaffolding gets precise, and stops fading.** You can support exactly the gap on exactly the day it appears. And nobody is watching to notice that the support never came off. Hence every scaffold in this repo requires a fade plan.
- **Choice needs calibrating, not maximising.** A class teacher restricts choice for manageability. You have no such limit, which means the risk runs the other way — an open field handed to a learner who freezes.
- **Momentum has no crowd behind it.** A quiet week in a class is absorbed. A quiet week with one learner is the project dying. This is what the support plan and check-in cadence are for.
