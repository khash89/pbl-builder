# Driving Questions

The driving question is the project's engine. Get it wrong and no amount of good design downstream compensates.

## What a good one does

It **frames a challenge** the learner has to resolve, and **cannot be resolved without learning the target standards.**

Both halves matter. A question that's genuinely open but doesn't require the content produces an engaged learner who learns nothing you planned. A question that requires the content but has one answer produces a research report.

## Five tests

**1. Is it open-ended?**
More than one defensible answer. If the answer is findable, it's a research prompt.

> ✗ "What are the parts of a watershed?"
> ✓ "How can we stop our driveway washing into the creek?"

**2. Would this learner understand and care about it?**
Read it aloud. If it sounds like a curriculum document, rewrite it in their words. A nine-year-old should be able to repeat it from memory.

> ✗ "How do anthropogenic factors influence local hydrological systems?"
> ✓ "Where does our water go, and who does it affect?"

**3. Does answering it require the standards?**
Take your standards list. Could a learner produce a decent answer without learning them? Then the standards are decoration.

**4. Is it a challenge, not a topic?**
Topics wearing question marks are the most common failure mode. The tell is that it names a subject rather than posing a problem.

> ✗ "How do local governments work?"
> ✓ "What should our town do with the empty lot on Main Street?"

**5. Is the learner in it?**
"We," "our," "I." Not always necessary, but its absence is often a sign the learner has no stake.

> ✗ "How do communities manage water resources?"
> ✓ "How can we keep our creek clean?"

## Question shapes that work

| Shape | Template | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Design** | How can we design/build/make X so that Y? | How can we build a shelter that survives a night out? |
| **Decision** | What should [body] do about X? | What should our town do with the empty lot? |
| **Position** | Should X? / Where should we draw the line on X? | Where should our school draw the line on AI? |
| **Improvement** | How can we improve X for Y? | How can we make the library work better for people who don't read English? |
| **Investigation** | Why does X happen here and not there? | Why does the flooding hit our street and not the next one? |
| **Explanation for an audience** | How can we explain X to Y so they can Z? | How can we explain diabetes to eight-year-olds so they'd notice it in a friend? |

The last one is underused and excellent — it makes the audience structural rather than an add-on.

## Fixing a weak one

**"What is photosynthesis?"** → closed, topic-shaped.
Ask *why does this matter to somebody*. → *"Why is the school garden failing on the north side?"*

**"How does government work?"** → topic, no learner stake.
Find a live local decision. → *"Should our town keep the crossing guard?"*

**"How can we help the environment?"** → too broad to be answerable, so it produces posters.
Narrow to something with a boundary you can see. → *"How can we cut what our house sends to landfill by half?"*

**"How do anthropogenic factors influence hydrological systems?"** → correct and unusable.
Say it the way you'd say it to them.

**"Should we recycle?"** → the answer is known, so it's not open.
Find the genuine tension. → *"Our town's recycling costs more than it saves. Should we keep it?"*

## The learner writes it

The strongest version: after the entry event and the need-to-know list, the learner drafts the driving question themselves. You refine it together.

Realistic by grade band:

- **Pre-K–2:** you write it, in their words, and they learn to say it
- **3–5:** you draft two or three, they choose and adjust
- **6–12:** they draft it from the need-to-know list; you push on openness and alignment

A learner who wrote the question owns the project differently. It's worth the extra day.

## With one learner

**You can tune it to one person, which is a real advantage.** A class question has to work for thirty different sets of interests. Yours has to work for one, whose interests you know precisely. Use it: put their actual obsession in the question.

**The community scale is available.** A class of thirty investigating one driveway is absurd. One learner investigating one driveway is exactly right — and it's more authentic than any classroom-scale question, because the problem is genuinely theirs and the finding genuinely matters to someone.

**Beware the question you want answered.** With one learner it's easy for the driving question to become a slightly disguised version of your own preoccupation — the thing you wish they cared about. It won't work, and it won't be obvious to you. Test: did their questions after the entry event actually point here, or did you steer?

## Common mistakes

- **Googleable.** The most common.
- **A topic with a question mark.** The second most common.
- **Too broad to answer.** Produces awareness-raising posters.
- **Written in curriculum language.** They can't hold it in mind.
- **Written after the activity.** Then it's a label, not an engine.
- **Never revisited.** It should be on the [project wall](10-strategies/project-wall.md) and referred to weekly. A driving question mentioned once in week one isn't driving anything.

## Checklist

- [ ] More than one defensible answer
- [ ] The learner could repeat it from memory and would want to know
- [ ] Answering it requires the target standards
- [ ] It poses a problem, not a subject
- [ ] The learner is implicated in it
- [ ] It's on the wall, and you refer to it weekly
