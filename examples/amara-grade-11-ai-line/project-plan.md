# Where Should We Draw The Line?

## Project Summary

Nobody at Amara's school can produce the AI policy. Teachers apply it
inconsistently and students describe it incompatibly. Over eight weeks she traces
the actual policy text to a dated board record, designs and pilots her own survey
of students and teachers, measures the gap between what the policy says and what
people believe it says, audits five published claims about student AI use against
criteria she writes, and recommends a specific revision — presenting it to the
school board's curriculum subcommittee with her methodology and her limitations
stated. Alongside it she keeps a decision log recording every consequential
choice she made, and in week nine she presents one she got wrong.

## Why This Project For This Learner

**The real goal is not statistics.** Amara will be fine academically anywhere. What
she has never done is choose. Everything she has done well was assigned, and she
has concluded that a task without instructions is a task she might fail — so she
waits. Given a free research week she produced twelve pages of immaculate notes on
the first subtopic she met, never chose a direction, and emailed four times asking
if she was doing it right. When I said it was up to her she said "but what would
you do?" She wasn't deflecting; the openness genuinely distressed her.

So this project is built to produce about twenty consequential decisions, and to
document all of them.

**Every choice is framed as a decision with criteria.** This is the design move
the whole thing rests on. The free-research week failed because it asked her to
*explore* — a thing she can't yet do. But she keeps a spreadsheet ranking every
teacher she has had against criteria she invented and weighted, and she can defend
the weighting. Judgment against constructed criteria is already hers. So: here are
the options, write your criteria, weight them, choose, record what it cost. Same
move, turned on her own project.

**The decision log is the scaffold, the assessment, and a product.** It starts as
a template with prompts, becomes her own format, and by week seven it's a
deliverable she presents from. It doesn't fade to nothing — it graduates. By week
eight it's documented evidence that she made twenty decisions and the project
survived all of them, including the bad ones. That evidence is the actual outcome.

**I have pre-committed to not answering.** From week two, "what would you do?" gets
"what are your criteria?" She agreed to this in advance and can hold me to having
warned her — which matters, because she asks persuasively and my own resolve is not
a reliable mechanism. I've also told her I'm counting how often she asks. The
baseline was four emails in one week; the count is the headline measure of the
success skill.

**The choice menu narrows deliberately and fades on named gates.** Her appetite is
low and this is her first project, so weeks 1–2 offer two or three named options.
Then three gates: after her first lived-with decision the survey design opens
fully; after the pilot forces a revision the analysis is hers; in week six the
presentation is hers. **She knows the gates and can see the schedule.** The
narrowing is scaffolding, not a verdict on her capability, and saying so out loud
is part of it.

**The statistics standard needs her to own the sample.** She can already calculate
a mean; she has never thought about who was in the sample. `HSS-IC.A.1` is about
inference to a population, and the only way she'll feel that is to write her own
sampling plan, discover in the pilot that it's flawed, and have to state in print
what her data cannot support about 900 students. Her brother's job is to find the
flaw before the subcommittee does.

**Symmetric scepticism is the sub-goal I'm most interested in.** She dismantles a
badly-sampled statistic in a news article and accepts a well-presented one that
agrees with her. So the source audit requires five claims *including at least one
she agrees with*, and the limitations section gets written before the findings, on
purpose. The sentence where she says what she wanted the data to show and didn't
find is the single best piece of evidence in the whole project.

**The presentation targets improvisation, not nerves.** Her audience comfort is
medium — she isn't frightened of people, she's frightened of improvising, and
under pressure she retreats into a script and reads it. So the ramp escalates
*hostility and unpredictability* rather than exposure: brother attacking the
sampling, then the teacher, then a hostile-question rehearsal. And the script is
reduced to a single card as a hard requirement. If she can't answer an
unanticipated question by the third rehearsal, the fix is cutting prepared
content, not adding more.

**What I made hard on purpose.** No adult support between sessions. Self-direction
cannot develop with someone always available — the gap is where it has to happen,
and I've told her the gap is deliberate rather than an accident of my schedule.

**The tripwire.** If by end of week three she hasn't made one decision without
asking me first, this is an assigned task with extra steps and I need to say so
and shrink the scope so the decisions get smaller and more frequent. Writing that
down now, before I'm invested in the brief being good.

## Week-by-Week Plan

Five hours a week: two 90-minute sessions with me plus about two hours alone. The
alone hours are the important ones and they are deliberately unsupported.

The subcommittee meets the 3rd Thursday, so week 9 is fixed and everything works
back from it. There is a week of slack in the middle because survey distribution
depends on other people.

### Week 1 — Launch

| Session | What happens | Ready when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The four documents. One question: what is the rule at your school? | She's noticed the three teachers disagree |
| 1 | Search board minutes for the policy's origin | Actual text, quoted, with a date |
| 2 | Need-to-know list. She drafts the driving question. | The question is hers |
| 2 | **Decision 1** — which aspect of the policy to focus on. Three options, criteria template. | Criteria written *before* the choice |
| 2 | Mini-lesson: a sample is a claim about people you didn't ask. 15 min. | — |

### Weeks 2–3 — Build Knowledge: design

| | What happens | Ready when |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Mini-lesson: surveys vs. observational studies; where randomisation enters | — |
| 2.1 | **GATE 1 OPENS** — survey design fully hers, no menu | She notices the menu is gone |
| 2.2 | Draft the instrument. Operationalise "cheating", or measure the disagreement about it. | The definitional problem is confronted, not dodged |
| 3.1 | Sampling plan: who, how many, recruited how — **and who is excluded** | Exclusions stated |
| 3.2 | **Brother's hostile review** of the design | Flaw found; her response logged as fix / defend / accept |

**Week 3 tripwire:** has she made a decision without asking me first? If not, stop and restructure.

### Week 4 — Build Knowledge: pilot

## Notes & Adaptations

*To be filled in while it runs.*

Things I already expect to have to adapt:

- **Survey distribution depends on other people**, which is why the slack is in
  week 5. If the school refuses, the brief becomes a policy-text-and-source-audit
  document presented to a department meeting. That still works and I should not
  treat it as a loss.
- **The "what would you do?" count.** If it isn't falling by week 4, the gates are
  opening too fast and I should narrow the next one rather than pushing her
  through.
- **Step 4 of the stuck protocol** — "write down what you're afraid will happen if
  you choose wrong" — is the one I expect to matter most and the one I'm least
  sure about. If it produces nothing twice, I make the decision visibly and hand
  the next one back rather than letting a fifth day of paralysis happen.
- **The script.** She will want to keep it. The single-card requirement is
  non-negotiable and I should expect to have this argument twice.
- **Her brother.** If he's too gentle, the design flaw survives to the
  subcommittee. Worth briefing him in week 2 that his job is to be hard on it,
  and that being hard on it is the help.
