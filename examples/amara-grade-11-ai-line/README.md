# Amara · Grade 11 · "Where Should We Draw The Line?"

> *Where should our school draw the line on AI, and what do the people it applies to actually think?*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Grade** | 11 |
| **Setting** | Tutoring, twice weekly |
| **Subjects** | Mathematics (statistics), English Language Arts |
| **Duration** | 8 weeks × 5 hours |
| **Target skill** | Self-directed learning — `self-regulation`, `autonomy` |
| **Product** | Data brief + recommendation; decision log |
| **Audience** | The school board's curriculum subcommittee |

## What this example demonstrates

**A high-achieving learner who has never chosen anything.** Amara does assigned work excellently and then stops. Given a free week to research she produced twelve immaculate pages on the first subtopic she met, never chose a direction, and emailed four times asking if she was doing it right. Asked to decide for herself she said "but what would you do?" — genuinely distressed by the openness, not deflecting.

She'll be fine academically anywhere. The thing she's never done is make a consequential decision and live with it.

**Reframing every choice as a decision with criteria.** This is the design move the whole project rests on. The free-research week failed because it asked her to *explore*. But she keeps a spreadsheet ranking every teacher she's had against criteria she invented and weighted, and can defend the weighting. Judgment against constructed criteria is already hers — so every choice arrives as: here are the options, write your criteria, weight them, choose, record what it cost.

**A scaffold that graduates into a product.** The decision log starts as a template with prompts, becomes her own format, and by week 7 is a deliverable she presents from — including one decision she got wrong. By week 8 it's documented evidence that she made twenty consequential decisions and the project survived all of them.

**An adult who pre-commits to not answering.** From week 2, "what would you do?" gets "what are your criteria?" She agreed in advance and can hold the tutor to having warned her — which matters, because she asks persuasively and willpower is not a mechanism. The count of how often she asks is the headline measure of the success skill.

**A fade schedule with named gates the learner can see.** Her choice appetite is low, so weeks 1–2 offer narrow menus. Then three gates widen it — after her first lived-with decision, after the pilot forces a revision, and in week 6. She knows what the gates are.

**Original data collection, with her owning the sampling.** `HSS-IC.A.1` is about inference to a population, and the only way she'll feel it is to write her own sampling plan, discover in a pilot that it's flawed, and state in print what her data cannot support about 900 students. Her brother — a statistics undergraduate — is briefed to find the flaw before the subcommittee does.

**Symmetric scepticism as an explicit target.** She dismantles a badly-sampled statistic she disagrees with and accepts a well-presented one she agrees with. So the source audit requires five claims *including one she agrees with*, and the limitations section is written before the findings.

**A ramp that escalates hostility, not exposure.** Her audience comfort is medium — she isn't frightened of people, she's frightened of improvising, and retreats into a script. So the rehearsals get progressively more hostile, and reducing the script to a single card is a hard requirement.

## How to adapt it

- **Different policy?** Any rule that affects the learner and is inconsistently applied: phone policy, dress code, homework, late work, a club's rules. The requirements are a real policy, a real inconsistency, and a body that can revise it.
- **No survey access?** The plan's `when_to_stop` covers this — it becomes a policy-text-and-source-audit brief presented to a department meeting. Still works.
- **Different target skill?** The same project supports critical thinking (`evaluation-of-claims`) very naturally. Pick one, not both.
- **No statistics undergraduate in the family?** Any numerate adult briefed to be hard on the sampling. A local pollster, a nurse, an accountant, an online stats community.
- **Shorter?** Drop the survey and audit five published claims instead. Keeps the statistics standards partly, loses the best part.
