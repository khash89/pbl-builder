# Critical Thinking

> Making reasoned judgments — working out what evidence means, drawing conclusions that the evidence actually supports, and evaluating the quality of claims including your own.

## Explanation & Analysis

`explanation-and-analysis` — *Can they take something apart and say how it works?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Restates what a source said. Notices details but treats them as a list rather than as connected parts. |
| **Emerging** | Summarises accurately and picks out which details matter. Begins to explain a cause or a relationship, usually one at a time. |
| **Developing** | Explains how parts of a system relate, using more than one step of reasoning. Notices patterns across sources. |
| **Demonstrating** | Builds a chain of reasoning that uncovers relationships, patterns, and inconsistencies — including cases that don't fit the pattern, and says so rather than smoothing them over. |

**How to teach it**

- Ask "and then what happens?" repeatedly until the chain runs out
- Require them to find one thing that doesn't fit their own explanation
- Use the What Makes You Say That? routine on their own claims, not just sources

**What tells you it's working:** Unprompted use of "because" and "which means" — and the harder signal, voluntarily raising a counterexample to their own point.

## Inference & Use of Evidence

`inference-and-evidence` — *Do their conclusions actually follow from what they found?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | States a conclusion. Evidence appears alongside it but isn't clearly connected to it, or is a single source taken at face value. |
| **Emerging** | Draws a conclusion and cites evidence for it. Tends to treat all sources as equally reliable. |
| **Developing** | Draws conclusions from multiple sources and begins to weigh them, noticing when sources disagree. |
| **Demonstrating** | Draws conclusions the evidence genuinely supports — no further — and states the limits: what this evidence can't tell us, and what would change the conclusion. |

**How to teach it**

- Ask: what would have to be true for this conclusion to be wrong?
- Give them two sources that conflict and require them to resolve it
- Require a stated limitation on every finding in the final product

**What tells you it's working:** Hedging that is precise rather than vague — "this only covers one summer, so I can't say it's a trend" is the target.

## Evaluation of Claims & Sources

`evaluation-of-claims` — *Can they judge whether something is worth believing?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Accepts sources as true or rejects them on surface features. Judgments rest on whether the claim matches what they already think. |
| **Emerging** | Identifies obvious strengths and weaknesses in an argument. Recognises that sources have authors with purposes. |
| **Developing** | Applies criteria — currency, expertise, method, agenda — fairly consistently. Identifies bias in others' claims. |
| **Demonstrating** | Evaluates against multiple criteria, identifies bias including in sources they agree with, and seeks out the strongest version of an opposing view rather than the weakest. |

**How to teach it**

- Have them argue the position they disagree with, well
- Ask "who benefits if I believe this?" of every source
- Have them audit their own product for bias before it goes public

**What tells you it's working:** Applying scepticism symmetrically. Most learners are sceptical of sources they dislike; the skill is being sceptical of the convenient ones.

## Problem Framing

`problem-framing` — *Can they work out what the real question is?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Accepts the problem exactly as handed to them. |
| **Emerging** | Restates the problem in their own words and identifies what they'd need to know to address it. |
| **Developing** | Breaks a large problem into tractable sub-questions and identifies which matter most. |
| **Demonstrating** | Reframes the problem when the original framing is wrong or too narrow, and can justify the reframing. |

**How to teach it**

- Ask: is this the right question, or just the first question?
- Have them write three versions of the driving question and choose
- Point at a dead end and ask what the question should have been

**What tells you it's working:** A learner who says "I think we're asking the wrong thing" — the most valuable and least common move on this rubric.

## What the project has to supply

This skill cannot grow without these. If the project does not provide them,
change the project or change the skill.

- A driving question with genuinely competing defensible answers
- Sources that disagree with each other
- A product that requires justifying a position, not just reporting findings
- An audience who might push back

**Products that suit it:** `policy-proposal`, `idea-talk`, `infographic-or-data-brief`, `documentary-or-video`, `business-or-venture-plan` — see [product-types.yaml](../../framework/product-types.yaml).

**Where it goes wrong:** A project where the answer is known in advance cannot develop critical thinking, however well designed the rest of it is. If you're targeting this skill, check the driving question first.

## Using it

- **The learner self-assesses first**, in their own words, before you say anything.
  The gap between their judgement and yours is the most useful data in the project.
- **Same task, twice** — first week and last week, on the same prompt, so the
  comparison means something. Show them both.
- **One level of growth in a project is a real result.** Two is a lot.
- **Assess it separately from the content.** A learner can produce an excellent
  product with weak skill, and the reverse.

*Research basis: The widely used consensus definition (Facione, Delphi Report, 1990) describes critical thinking as purposeful, self-regulatory judgment involving interpretation, analysis, evaluation, and inference — and, importantly, as a disposition as much as a procedure: habitually inquisitive, willing to reconsider, honest about one's own biases.*
