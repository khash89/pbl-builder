# Creativity & Innovation

> Producing something that is both novel and useful — new relative to what the learner had seen, and genuinely fit for the purpose it was made for.

**Pick one or two dimensions from this rubric — not all of them.** Everything you do not name is explicitly out of scope for this project, and saying so out loud is part of keeping the focus real.

---

## A common misreading

Creativity is not artistic talent, and it is not decoration. A learner can score highly on this rubric with a spreadsheet, a survey design, or a mechanism, and score poorly with a beautiful drawing that copies its model. If your assessment reduces to "how pretty is it," you are assessing something else.

## Generating & Exploring Ideas

`idea-generation` — *Do they produce real options before committing?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Produces one idea and starts building it. Ideas that do appear may not connect to the actual problem. |
| **Emerging** | Generates a few ideas, mostly variations on the first one or on an example they've seen. |
| **Developing** | Generates a genuine range of ideas connected to the objectives, and holds off committing until several are on the table. |
| **Demonstrating** | Generates ideas from more than one angle, combines and adapts them into options that didn't exist at the start, and can explain why they set aside the ones they set aside. |

**How to teach it**

- Require five options before any building begins
- What would this look like if it were the opposite?
- Look at how a different field solves the same structural problem

**What tells you it's working:** A later idea that clearly descends from combining two earlier ones. That's the mechanism working.

## Novelty & Usefulness of the Product

`novelty-and-usefulness` — *Is it both new and fit for purpose?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Closely follows an example or the conventional approach. Works, but contains no decision that couldn't be predicted. |
| **Emerging** | Recognisably conventional with some original elements, usually surface-level. Or original but doesn't quite do the job. |
| **Developing** | Contains genuinely original choices that serve the purpose. Someone familiar with the conventional approach would notice the difference. |
| **Demonstrating** | Both novel and useful — an approach the learner arrived at themselves that solves the problem better than the obvious approach would, and demonstrably works. |

**How to teach it**

- Show the conventional solution and ask what it gets wrong
- Add a constraint that makes the obvious approach impossible
- Test the product with real users and revise on what breaks

**What tells you it's working:** Usefulness verified by contact with reality, not asserted. Did they test it on anyone?

**Note:** Novelty is judged relative to this learner's experience, not to world history. A nine-year-old reinventing a known technique independently is demonstrating creativity.

## Explaining Creative Choices

`explaining-choices` — *Can they account for what they did?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Can't say why. "I just liked it," or attributes the choice to how it turned out. |
| **Emerging** | Gives a reason, usually about preference rather than purpose. |
| **Developing** | Explains choices in terms of what the product needs to do and who it's for. |
| **Demonstrating** | Articulates the reasoning behind decisions, including the trade-offs accepted and the alternatives rejected, in terms of purpose and audience. |

**How to teach it**

- "Why this and not the other one?" on every significant decision
- Keep a running decision log with reasons written at the time
- Have them defend a choice to someone who prefers the alternative

**What tells you it's working:** Naming a trade-off — "I lost some accuracy but a ten-year-old can actually read it." That's design thinking.

## Risk-Taking & Iteration

`risk-and-iteration` — *Do they try things that might not work?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Avoids anything uncertain. Sticks to what's guaranteed to be adequate. |
| **Emerging** | Will try something new when encouraged and supported. Abandons it quickly if the first attempt fails. |
| **Developing** | Chooses to try uncertain approaches. Iterates when they fail rather than reverting to the safe option. |
| **Demonstrating** | Deliberately pursues the ambitious version, treats failed attempts as information, and can point to how a specific failure improved the final product. |

**How to teach it**

- Make one required attempt explicitly non-assessed
- Ask what the ambitious version would be, then ask what it would take
- Require the final product to document one thing that didn't work

**What tells you it's working:** Voluntarily discarding something that already worked in order to try something better. Rare and worth naming out loud when it happens.

## What the project has to supply

This skill cannot grow without these. If the project does not provide them,
change the project or change the skill.

- A problem with several genuinely viable solutions
- Time and permission for at least one attempt to fail
- Assessment criteria about fitness for purpose, not polish
- Real users or an audience to test usefulness against

**Products that suit it:** `physical-model`, `picture-book-or-zine`, `public-service-announcement`, `event`, `interactive-map-or-tour`, `documentary-or-video` — see [product-types.yaml](../../framework/product-types.yaml).

**Where it goes wrong:** If the product has a template or a single conventional form, creativity has nowhere to live. Also: don't target creativity in a compressed timeline — without room to iterate, you'll be assessing the first idea, which is exactly what this rubric penalises.

## Using it

- **The learner self-assesses first**, in their own words, before you say anything.
  The gap between their judgement and yours is the most useful data in the project.
- **Same task, twice** — first week and last week, on the same prompt, so the
  comparison means something. Show them both.
- **One level of growth in a project is a real result.** Two is a lot.
- **Assess it separately from the content.** A learner can produce an excellent
  product with weak skill, and the reverse.
