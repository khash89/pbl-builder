# Self-Directed Learning

> Managing your own learning: setting goals, getting started, keeping going when it's hard, noticing when you're off track, and taking responsibility for the outcome.

**Pick one or two dimensions from this rubric — not all of them.** Everything you do not name is explicitly out of scope for this project, and saying so out loud is part of keeping the focus real.

---

## With one learner

This is the most commonly chosen skill in one-learner settings, and the hardest to grow there, because the adult is always available. The support plan matters more than the rubric: if you answer every question immediately, you cannot assess self-direction, because you've removed the conditions for it. Deliberate unavailability is an instructional choice.

## Goal-Setting & Self-Regulation

`self-regulation` — *Can they set a target and steer themselves toward it?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Works to goals set by an adult. Needs a prompt to begin and another to resume after an interruption. Doesn't notice drift from the plan. |
| **Emerging** | Sets short-term goals when asked, and meets them with reminders. Notices being behind, usually after the fact. |
| **Developing** | Sets their own goals for a work session and mostly hits them. Notices drift during the work and corrects. Asks for help at roughly the right time. |
| **Demonstrating** | Sets goals that are ambitious but achievable, tracks their own progress against them, and adjusts the plan when reality changes — then can explain what they changed and why. |

**How to teach it**

- Learner writes the session goal before starting and reviews it after
- Estimate-then-check on task duration (see scaffolds.yaml)
- [object Object]

**What tells you it's working:** Self-correction without prompting. Not "did they finish" — did they notice and adjust.

## Persistence & Frustration Tolerance

`persistence` — *What happens when it gets hard?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Stops when stuck. Frustration ends the session. Difficulty is read as evidence they can't do it. |
| **Emerging** | Keeps going with encouragement. Will try again after a break. Still interprets difficulty as a signal about their ability. |
| **Developing** | Uses a strategy when stuck rather than stopping, and tries more than one thing before asking. Recovers from setbacks within the session. |
| **Demonstrating** | Treats difficulty as expected and informative. Has a repertoire of strategies and chooses between them. Can name what they learned from something that failed. |

**How to teach it**

- A posted stuck ladder, used out loud until it's internal
- Tell them about your own failures on the project, specifically
- Praise the strategy, never the ease — "you tried three things" not "that was quick"

**What tells you it's working:** Language shift from "I can't" to "I haven't yet found." Real, and observable.

## Autonomy & Initiative

`autonomy` — *Do they act without being told to?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Waits for direction. Asks what to do next even when the plan says. |
| **Emerging** | Follows an agreed plan independently. Asks before deviating, even trivially. |
| **Developing** | Recognises the choices available and makes them. Starts work without prompting. Brings problems with a proposed solution attached. |
| **Demonstrating** | Takes charge of their own learning — seeks resources and people you didn't suggest, proposes changes to the plan, and pursues questions past the requirement because they want the answer. |

**How to teach it**

- Answer questions with questions for a defined period, and tell them that's what you're doing
- Require a proposed solution to accompany every problem raised
- Be deliberately unavailable for a scheduled block

**What tells you it's working:** Resources or contacts in the final product that you never mentioned. That's the clearest evidence there is.

## Personal Responsibility

`responsibility` — *Do they own the outcome?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Attributes setbacks to circumstances, other people, or the task being unfair. |
| **Emerging** | Acknowledges their part in a setback when it's pointed out. Follow-through is inconsistent. |
| **Developing** | Names their own contribution to both problems and successes. Meets commitments; tells you in advance when they won't. |
| **Demonstrating** | Takes responsibility without being asked, including for work that wasn't good enough. Distinguishes what was in their control from what wasn't, accurately and without either excuse-making or self-blame. |

**How to teach it**

- Model it — say when you got something wrong in the project
- "What was in your control here?" as a routine question, asked neutrally
- Let a missed deadline have its natural consequence

**What tells you it's working:** Accuracy, not contrition. A learner who takes the blame for everything is failing this dimension in the other direction.

## What the project has to supply

This skill cannot grow without these. If the project does not provide them,
change the project or change the skill.

- Genuine open space where the learner decides what happens next
- Long enough that early decisions have visible later consequences
- An adult who is deliberately not always available
- Learner-owned management tools, not adult-owned ones

**Products that suit it:** `event`, `business-or-venture-plan`, `documentary-or-video`, `interactive-map-or-tour`, `physical-model` — see [product-types.yaml](../../framework/product-types.yaml).

**Where it goes wrong:** A tightly scaffolded project with a fixed daily sequence can't develop self-direction — there's nothing to direct. If the learner also has low choice appetite, target one dimension only (usually self-regulation) and widen the space deliberately across the project.

## Using it

- **The learner self-assesses first**, in their own words, before you say anything.
  The gap between their judgement and yours is the most useful data in the project.
- **Same task, twice** — first week and last week, on the same prompt, so the
  comparison means something. Show them both.
- **One level of growth in a project is a real result.** Two is a lot.
- **Assess it separately from the content.** A learner can produce an excellent
  product with weak skill, and the reverse.
