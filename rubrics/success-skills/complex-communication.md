# Complex Communication

> Understanding what others are actually saying, and making yourself understood — in words, in writing, in how you carry yourself, and with the tools you choose — adapted to who you're talking to.

## Synthesis & Clarity

`synthesis-and-clarity` — *Can they make something complicated understandable?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Conveys the main idea and some details. Organisation follows the order they learned things rather than the order the listener needs. Vocabulary is general where it should be precise. |
| **Emerging** | Organised and followable. Uses key technical terms, sometimes without being sure of them. Reports what sources said more than what it means. |
| **Developing** | Organised for the listener. Synthesises multiple sources into a single line of thought and uses technical vocabulary accurately. |
| **Demonstrating** | Structures the message around what the audience needs to understand first. Interprets and evaluates rather than reporting, uses precise language, and draws conclusions the audience can follow all the way through. |

**How to teach it**

- "Say it in one sentence" — repeatedly, until the sentence is good
- Have them explain it to someone genuinely uninformed and watch where confusion lands
- Cut the first minute of any talk; it's almost always throat-clearing

**What tells you it's working:** Reordering their own material for the listener's benefit rather than keeping it in the order they discovered it.

## Audience Awareness

`audience-awareness` — *Do they adapt to who's actually in front of them?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Same delivery regardless of audience. Assumes shared background knowledge that the audience doesn't have. |
| **Emerging** | Recognises the audience differs and makes surface adjustments — mostly formality, and mostly by removing jargon. |
| **Developing** | Adjusts vocabulary, examples, and level of detail for the audience. Anticipates the obvious questions. |
| **Demonstrating** | Works out what this audience cares about and builds from there. Anticipates objections and addresses them unprompted. Adapts in the moment when the room isn't following. |

**How to teach it**

- "Who is in the room and what do they already believe?" before drafting
- Have them prepare the same content for two different audiences
- Rehearse with someone who will genuinely push back

**What tells you it's working:** Reading the room mid-delivery and changing course. Advanced, and unmistakable when it happens.

## Non-Verbal Communication

`non-verbal` — *Does their body support what they're saying?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Posture and eye contact undercut the message. Reads from notes throughout. Visible discomfort dominates. |
| **Emerging** | Some eye contact. Volume and pace inconsistent. Notes are a crutch but not the whole delivery. |
| **Developing** | Generally audible, paced, and connected to the audience. Uses gesture purposefully at least sometimes. |
| **Demonstrating** | Delivery reinforces the content — pace slows at the hard part, gesture illustrates structure, silence is used deliberately, eye contact is distributed and genuine. |

**How to teach it**

- Record a rehearsal and watch it together with the sound off
- Practise one thing at a time — volume this round, pausing the next
- [object Object]

**What tells you it's working:** Deliberate pausing. It's the last thing to develop and the clearest sign of comfort.

**Note:** Be careful here with a learner for whom eye contact or stillness is genuinely difficult rather than merely unpractised. Assess whether the delivery serves the message, not whether it matches a neurotypical default. Adjust the descriptors and say so in the plan.

## Strategic Use of Tools & Media

`tool-use` — *Do their slides, visuals, and tech help or hurt?*

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Media distracts from the message — text-dense slides read aloud, decorative visuals, tech that fails. |
| **Emerging** | Media is present and doesn't actively harm. Visuals mostly duplicate what's being said. |
| **Developing** | Visuals carry information the words can't, and are chosen for the format. |
| **Demonstrating** | Every element is doing work the speaker can't do alone, chosen deliberately for this audience and this room — including the choice to use nothing. |

**How to teach it**

- Ban slides for the first rehearsal entirely
- "What is this slide doing that you can't say?" — delete it if there's no answer
- Test the tech in the actual room, in advance

**What tells you it's working:** Choosing not to use a tool because it wouldn't help. Genuinely sophisticated.

## What the project has to supply

This skill cannot grow without these. If the project does not provide them,
change the project or change the skill.

- A real audience that isn't obliged to be interested
- Enough rehearsal built into the calendar to actually improve
- Feedback separated into content first, delivery later
- Content complex enough that clarity is a real achievement

**Products that suit it:** `idea-talk`, `policy-proposal`, `podcast`, `picture-book-or-zine`, `exhibit-or-installation`, `documentary-or-video` — see [product-types.yaml](../../framework/product-types.yaml).

**Where it goes wrong:** Don't target this skill and then let the presentation be the last thing planned. It needs three rehearsals in the calendar and separate content and delivery feedback rounds. If those aren't scheduled, choose a different skill.

## Using it

- **The learner self-assesses first**, in their own words, before you say anything.
  The gap between their judgement and yours is the most useful data in the project.
- **Same task, twice** — first week and last week, on the same prompt, so the
  comparison means something. Show them both.
- **One level of growth in a project is a real result.** Two is a lot.
- **Assess it separately from the content.** A learner can produce an excellent
  product with weak skill, and the reverse.
